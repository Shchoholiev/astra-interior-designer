# Lived-in interior styling

Use after the major layout is credible when creating or improving a finished residential interior, especially when a technically detailed room still feels empty or uninhabited. Treat signs of daily use as part of the design brief, alongside geometry, materials, and lighting. A simple kitchen can have a simple layout and still feel like someone lives there.

Respect the requested stage and scope. Defer this pass during a layout-only blockout. An explicitly empty room, restrained showroom, or bare-surface brief overrides the residential default. Minimalist homes need fewer, carefully chosen objects, not automatic clutter. A render-only, material-only, or exact-scene export request does not authorize new furnishings; identify missing styling as a limitation when additions are outside scope.

## Plan for the people using the room

Before sourcing props, add a short styling plan to the task's local progress record: intended daily activities, visible zones, proposed object groups and their purpose, surfaces to keep clear, and the reference's density and palette. Use ordinary assumptions appropriate to the brief; do not invent a resident's personal history. For reconstruction, follow the actual reference's objects, grouping, and negative space instead of substituting a generic decor kit.

For a kitchen, consider preparation, cooking, washing, drinks, storage, and eating. Select a few coherent groups suited to the available space and camera; the examples below are alternatives, not a shopping list to instantiate in every room.

| Zone | Possible signs of use | Placement to preserve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Preparation | Cutting board with a bowl of produce; a small oil/salt group | A usable clear preparation area and access to drawers |
| Cooking | Credible cookware and a nearby utensil holder | Burner space, handles, ventilation, and separation of textiles/plants from heat |
| Washing | Soap, a small dish, and a naturally draped towel | Sink/faucet access, dry electrical equipment, and realistic towel support |
| Drinks | Kettle or coffee maker with cups on a tray | Reachable controls, stable support, and plausible power access |
| Dining | A restrained setting for the intended diners, folded linen, or a shared serving bowl | Usable seats, legroom, and space to place dishes |
| Walls, shelves, and window area | Suitable art, cookbooks, storage jars, plant or flowers | Openings, usable shelf depth, daylight, and room circulation |

Balance functional objects with a few decorative or personal-looking details. Carry the room's material and color choices into ceramics, glassware, metal, wood, and textiles. Use differences in height, grouped relationships, and restrained asymmetry where natural; avoid isolated props at uniform intervals, identical rotations, or filling every surface. Broad quiet areas help groups read clearly. More objects, dirt, and wear are not automatic realism improvements.

## Build and place the chosen groups

Use [find-3d-models](../../find-3d-models/SKILL.md) for visible complex objects when an appropriate authored asset is available. A toy-like plant, featureless appliance, or flat dish can weaken the whole composition; simple geometry is sufficient only when its construction and silhouette hold up at the delivered distance. Source coherent assets rather than unrelated styles chosen solely because they are downloadable. Keep provenance and portable dependencies.

Place objects in relation to their use and support, not just in unoccupied bounding boxes. Follow [asset integration](asset-integration.md#support-and-assembly-connections) for actual contact and complete assemblies, and [kitchen construction](kitchen-construction.md) for tableware, hardware, and appliances. Check scale against cabinets and hands' likely reach, dish thickness, container contents, attached stems, and textile drape. Retain approved major furniture positions and architectural dimensions unless changing them is requested.

## Review the inhabited composition

Before the final render, inspect the complete camera image: does the room show plausible daily activities, and does its density match the brief or reference? Record missing or awkward groups as composition findings, alongside material and lighting findings. Then inspect relevant close-ups and a diagnostic top/side view for support, intersections, blocked work areas, and unusable seating. Compare a styling pass with the same camera and lighting so its contribution is visible. Correct dominant in-scope omissions or clutter before delivery; a single plant and empty surrounding surfaces do not establish a finished lived-in kitchen. Styling supports photorealism but does not compensate for poor geometry, surfaces, lighting, or camera placement.
